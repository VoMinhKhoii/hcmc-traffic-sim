01

Start here

You already know the brief: six intersections in Phú Nhuận, a railway crossing the network, traffic lights that adapt to conditions, priority vehicles, and failures to handle. This page is everything the team worked out from that brief — the design, the numbers behind it, and where the model is deliberately wrong.

What you have been given. This document, and the simulator. Between them you should be able to write the design sections of the report without reading a line of code.

What the simulator is. A design-discovery tool. It is not the graded deliverable — the QNX program is. The simulator exists to do three things: find the smallest set of messages the controllers need to exchange, produce evidence for every timing number so none of them is a guess, and let us demonstrate scenarios that would be impossible to stage on a real street.

What to take from it. Three tables the report needs — the messages, the timings, the failures. They are in sections 09, 10 and 08 below.

One thing to be careful about

The simulator runs every controller inside a single program. That is enough to prove these messages are sufficient. It is not enough to prove these deadlines are met under load — that claim needs the real QNX build, with real scheduling. Do not let the report blur the two.

The network

Six signalised intersections, seven road links, and a north–south railway that crosses the road network in three places. Distances are from the team's survey of the area and drive every timing number later in this document.

Two of the streets are main roads — Nguyễn Văn Trỗi and Trần Huy Liệu — and carry roughly two and a half times the traffic of the side streets. That split is why the green times are not equal.

03

How the traffic lights work

Each intersection runs one of three patterns, chosen by the time of day. The control room selects the pattern; the intersection always drives its own lights.

  • Peak (06:30–09:30, 16:00–19:00) — fixed coordinated plans. Green times are computed once from Webster's formula and held. Every intersection shares one 40-second cycle, and each is offset from the last by the driving time between them, so a car that catches one green catches the next. Varying the timing freely would break that.
  • Off-peak — vehicle-actuated. The green stays on while cars keep arriving and ends when a 3-second gap appears, subject to a 7-second minimum and a 40-second cap. A direction with nobody waiting is skipped entirely.
  • Night (23:00–04:00) — flashing. Amber on the main road, red on the side road. A pedestrian button summons one full cycle, then it returns to flashing.

Pedestrians cross alongside the parallel traffic green, so they cost no extra time. The minimum green is 12 seconds, which is how long it takes to walk a two-by-two-lane crossing at 1.2 m/s. At peak the walk signal appears every cycle automatically; off-peak it needs a button press, and a press will force a phase that would otherwise be skipped.

04

The railway

A train is the one thing on this network with an unmissable deadline. It cannot be asked to wait at a red light, and it cannot stop quickly, so everything else has to get out of its way in time.

The sequence starts 30 seconds before the train reaches a crossing, which at 60 km/h means the train is 500 metres away. Three things then have to happen in order: the cars trapped between the rails and the next traffic light have to be moved, the barrier has to come down, and the movements pointing at the tracks have to be held red until the train has gone.

The pocket, and why it needs flushing

In plain terms. Driving east along Nguyễn Văn Trỗi you cross the railway, then have 110 metres of road, then reach the traffic light at I5. That 110 metres is the pocket. When the light is red, cars queue back from it — and the first 31 fit. Car number 32 is standing on the railway. The flush is the fix: when a train is spotted, the light goes green for that direction early, out of turn, so the queue rolls forward and its tail comes off the rails before the barrier drops.
Why the flush exists at all

It looks like a safety patch, but it is not. Measured with the flush removed, the crossing at I5 loses its green every time a train comes — every two minutes at peak — and its share of green collapses from 33.5% to 21.8%. That is below what the traffic needs, so the queue grows without limit and then reaches the tracks. At double demand that is 3324 safety violations and a queue of 362 cars, against zero and 23 with the flush. The flush is what stops the railway from starving the main road.

The barrier

In plain terms. A barrier is only ever in one of three situations: the road is open, the road is closed for a train on its way, or the barrier is broken. The third is not simply "closed with a warning light on it" — a broken barrier means nobody can prove the road is protected, so the trains must be stopped too, and only a person can put it right.

Three rows that differ, so three states and no more. A barrier stuck down is the safe failure — road blocked, crossing protected, trains unaffected — and is deliberately left out of the model.

05

Priority vehicles

An ambulance is given a green wave. The operator picks a route at the control room, which works out when the vehicle will reach each intersection along it and tells each one separately. Every intersection then schedules its own safe transition so that its green is showing before the ambulance arrives, and holds it until told the vehicle has passed.

06

When traffic backs up

An intersection watches its own queues. If one stays above 25 vehicles for 20 seconds it reports the problem to the control room — it is not allowed to retime itself. Twenty seconds of patience stops the alarm chattering, and it only clears once the queue falls to 15, not 25, so it cannot flicker on the threshold.

The control room responds two ways at once. It gives the congested intersection more green for the direction that is backed up — up to 8 seconds moved from the other direction, with the cycle length untouched so the green wave survives. And where the congestion is fed by a neighbour, it throttles that neighbour's green so less traffic arrives.

You can watch this happen. Set the demand slider to ×1.5 at peak and the split at I5 visibly flexes from 15 seconds to 18 and back, with the alarm and the resulting change linked in the message pane.

07

When things break

Two failures are in scope, both required by the brief.

The control room goes offline. Press Kill central and nothing freezes. Every intersection keeps running the last plan it was given and stores its status reports until the link returns, then sends the backlog. This is the reason the intersections are separate programs rather than threads of the control room: if the control room were the only thing making decisions, losing it would stop six intersections at once.

A barrier fails. Press Jam gate A. Nothing happens immediately, which is correct — the arm is now physically stuck, but nobody has asked it to move yet. The fault appears at the next train, when the barrier is commanded down and fails to report back within ten seconds. The crossing then closes the road in both directions and keeps it closed, the train signal goes to danger and the approaching train brakes to a stand, and an alarm reaches the control room. A repair takes somewhere between five and fifteen minutes, after which everything returns to normal by itself.

Both failures degrade the service and neither creates a dangerous state. That distinction is the point: the system is allowed to become less useful, never less safe.

Worth saying in the report

Holding a main road closed for ten minutes builds a queue of several hundred vehicles. The model has no limit on how many cars a road can hold, so that queue is a number rather than a physical jam. It is a real limitation, not a display bug — see section 11.

08

How it is built

Every controller is a separate program that can only see two things: its own sensors, and messages sent to it. No controller reads another's memory. That restriction is the whole design, because it is what lets the same structure run as separate QNX processes — a process cannot reach into another process's memory even if it wanted to.

10

What we assumed

Every model is wrong in chosen ways. Naming them is what separates an engineering model from a toy, and a simplification you declared is a decision while the same one left unmentioned is a hole. There are 41 in total; these are the ones that change what you can honestly claim.

  • No spillback. A road has no limit on how many cars it can hold, so a full road never blocks the intersection feeding it. This is the biggest departure from reality and it flatters our results.
  • The simulator proves the messages, not the timing. All controllers run inside one program. "These nine messages are enough" carries over to QNX. "The deadline is met" does not.
  • Detectors are perfect. Queue readings are never noisy, stale or broken — yet every adaptive decision rests on one.
  • Queues are numbers, not cars. No lane changing, no following behaviour, no individual drivers.
  • Nobody turns. All traffic goes straight through, which is why two phases suffice.
  • Arrivals are steady, not random. Traffic arrives at a constant rate, so no delay statistics can be drawn from the output.
  • One gate fault only. Failure to close. A barrier stuck down is the safe failure and is excluded.
  • Saturation flow is calibrated, not measured. Raised for motorbike-dominated traffic with no field survey behind it.
  • Braking is smooth. No brake build-up, no driver reaction delay.
  • The link is up or down. No partial loss, corruption, reordering or duplication — a real QNX port has to decide what a failed send actually means.
11

Running and demonstrating it

Serve the folder over HTTP and open it — double-clicking the file will not work, because browsers block the module loading it uses. On macOS, ./run.command does it. If your changes do not appear, the browser has cached the old files; hard-reload.

Reading the screen. One dot is one vehicle, moving or waiting. The pane on the right shows every message with the measurement that caused it and the change it produced, with related messages tagged so you can see an alarm and the plan changes it triggered as one event. Click any intersection to see its own controller: what plan it is running, who told it to, and why.

A fifteen-minute demonstration, in order.

  1. Peak at 07:00, sped up 30×. Point out the green wave along Nguyễn Văn Trỗi into Trần Huy Liệu, the walk signal every cycle, and a train every two minutes taking all three crossings in turn.
  2. Send the ambulance from I1 to I4 and watch the greens open ahead of it.
  3. Drop an accident on I2 → I4. Queues build, the alarm fires, the control room retimes. Point at the message pane as it happens — the change in green time is on the same line as the alarm that caused it.
  4. Push demand to ×1.5 and watch a green split flex from 15 to 18 seconds and back.
  5. Jump to 11:00 for actuated control: gaps end greens early, empty directions are skipped.
  6. Jump to 23:30 for flashing mode, then force a train and watch an intersection come out of flashing into a full preemption and return.
  7. Kill the control room. Nothing stops. Restore it and the backlog flushes.
  8. Jam gate A. Nothing happens until the next train — then the crossing breaks, the road closes, and the train brakes to a stand short of the crossing.
12

Known gaps

An independent review of the implementation found three places where the code does not yet match the design described above. They are listed here rather than hidden, because you may be asked about them.

  • The hold on a broken crossing is released briefly. The design says a broken crossing holds the feeding traffic until repair. In fact each following train re-triggers the flush stage, giving that direction about 20 seconds of green on a crossing that is broken. The road stays closed throughout, so it is not a safety problem, but it is not what the design says.
  • A train joining the back of a queue can stop instantly. The leading train brakes properly. A train arriving behind a stopped queue is placed at full speed and then clamped, which produces an unrealistic instantaneous stop.
  • The barrier travel time is wired into the fault detection. Changing that one number would make every crossing fault on every train. In the QNX version this disappears, because the confirmation comes from a real sensor rather than a timer.

None of the three affects the safety results or the message design. All are fixable.
